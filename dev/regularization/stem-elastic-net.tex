\begin{abstract}
We add a \emph{cluster-specific} elastic-net penalty on the regression
coefficients of a hierarchical spatio-temporal state-space model with spatial
regimes, estimated by EM. The penalty leaves the E-step untouched, so the
algorithm remains an EM on the penalized observed-data likelihood; the M-step
for the coefficients keeps a closed form under a ridge and is solved exactly by
coordinate descent otherwise. Three features distinguish the problem from the
usual regularized regression. First, the relevant cross-product is a
generalized least squares one, carrying the inverse of an estimated spatial
covariance, so the scaling that makes the penalty comparable across covariates
is not the Euclidean standardization, and the response scale has to be removed
from one half of the penalty and not the other. Second, the penalty operates
within regimes whose size is itself a constraint of the procedure, which is
exactly where collinearity binds, and letting $(\alpha_g,\lambda_g)$ vary with
the regime turns the amount of shrinkage into something the data decide rather
than something imposed. Third, the four hyperparameters of the procedure ---
the number of regimes, the spatial penalty, and the two of the elastic net ---
are not separable, and we give the analysis of what depends on what. Penalized
linear regression is recovered as a special case by switching off the latent
process and the spatial correlation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation}

Two things make a penalty on the regression coefficients worth having in this
class of models, and neither is the usual high-dimensional argument.

The first is that the covariates of an environmental monitoring network are
strongly collinear by construction. Meteorological drivers are measured on
overlapping supports, chemical species share sources, and the design matrix of a
regional network routinely has condition numbers in the hundreds. The variance
of the maximum likelihood estimator of $\beta$ is governed by the inverse of
$M = \sum_t X_t'\Se^{-1}X_t$, and it is that inverse that a ridge stabilizes.

The second is specific to the clustered model. A spatially-clustered STEM model
fits a complete state-space model inside every regime; the regimes are subsets
of the network, and their size is bounded below by the requirement that each of
them support its own variance components and range. Collinearity that is
tolerable on a network of two hundred stations is not tolerable on a regime of
twelve, and the binding constraint on how many regimes may be entertained is,
in practice, the smallest one that can still be fitted. A penalty that reduces
the effective dimension of the regression inside a regime relaxes that
constraint directly.

\section{The model}

For $t = 1,\dots,T$ and $d$ locations with coordinates $s_1,\dots,s_d$,
\begin{align}
  z_t &= X_t\beta + K y_t + e_t, & e_t &\sim N_d(0,\Se), \label{eq:meas}\\
  y_t &= G\,y_{t-1} + \eta_t,    & \eta_t &\sim N_p(0,\Sig_\eta),
  \label{eq:state}
\end{align}
with $X_t$ the $d\times r$ design of period $t$, $K$ a known $d\times p$
loading matrix, $y_0 \sim N_p(m_0, C_0)$, and
\begin{equation}
  \Se = \sigma^2_\varepsilon I_d + \sigma^2_\omega\, C(h;\theta),
  \qquad C(h;\theta) = \exp(-\theta h),
  \label{eq:cov}
\end{equation}
$h$ the matrix of distances. Write
$\psi = (\beta,\sigma^2_\varepsilon,\sigma^2_\omega,\theta,G,\Sig_\eta,m_0)$.

The spatially-clustered model partitions the locations into $k$ regimes
$I_1,\dots,I_k$ and gives each of them its own $\psi_g$, the partition being
estimated jointly with the parameters by maximizing a Potts-penalized
likelihood. Everything below applies within a regime, so we develop it for the
pooled model and return to the clustered case in Section~\ref{sec:scstem}.

\section{The penalized objective}

Let $\ell(\psi)$ be the observed-data log-likelihood of \eqref{eq:meas}. We
consider
\begin{equation}
  \ell_\pi(\psi) = \ell(\psi) - \pi(\beta),
  \qquad
  \pi(\beta) = \lambda\left\{
    \alpha\,\lVert D\beta\rVert_1
    + \tfrac{1-\alpha}{2}\,\beta'D\beta \right\},
  \label{eq:pen}
\end{equation}
with $\lambda \ge 0$, $\alpha\in[0,1]$ and $D = \mathrm{diag}(w_1,\dots,w_r)$,
$w_j\in\{0,1\}$, the indicator of the penalized coordinates. This is the
parameterization of \citet{ZouHastie2005}: $\alpha=0$ is a ridge, $\alpha=1$ a
lasso, and anything in between an elastic net.

Only $\beta$ is penalized. The variance components, the range, the transition
matrix and the initial state are left at their maximum likelihood values. That
is a modelling decision and not a technical one: $\sigma^2_\varepsilon$,
$\sigma^2_\omega$ and $\theta$ describe the error process rather than the mean,
and shrinking them would change the object being estimated rather than
stabilize its estimation.

\subsection{The intercept}

The default takes $w_1 = 0$ for the intercept, and here the usual convention
carries a substantive reason. The state equation \eqref{eq:state} has an
initial mean $m_0$ which is estimated, and the latent process it starts feeds
into \eqref{eq:meas} additively through $K$. When $K = \mathbf{1}_d$, as it is
in the clustered model within each regime, the intercept and the level of the
latent process are identified only through the dynamics: a constant may be moved
from $\beta_0$ into $m_0$ and back with a compensating change in the transient.
Shrinking $\beta_0$ therefore does not shrink ``the level''; it moves the level
into $m_0$, at a rate that depends on $G$. The penalty would be doing something
other than what it appears to do.

\section{EM with a penalty}

Let $Q(\psi\mid\psi^{(i)}) = E_{\psi^{(i)}}[\,\ell_c(\psi)\mid z\,]$ be the
usual expected complete-data log-likelihood, the expectation taken over the
latent states $y_{0:T}$.

\begin{proposition}[The E-step is unchanged and the algorithm is monotone]
\label{prop:monotone}
Define the penalized EM map by
\begin{equation}
  \psi^{(i+1)} = \argmax_{\psi}\;\bigl\{ Q(\psi\mid\psi^{(i)}) - \pi(\beta) \bigr\}.
  \label{eq:mstep}
\end{equation}
Then $\ell_\pi(\psi^{(i+1)}) \ge \ell_\pi(\psi^{(i)})$ for every $i$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write $\ell(\psi) = Q(\psi\mid\psi^{(i)}) - H(\psi\mid\psi^{(i)})$ with
$H(\psi\mid\psi^{(i)}) = E_{\psi^{(i)}}[\log f(y_{0:T}\mid z,\psi)\mid z]$, and
recall $H(\psi\mid\psi^{(i)}) \le H(\psi^{(i)}\mid\psi^{(i)})$ by Jensen's
inequality. Since $\pi$ is a function of $\beta$ alone and does not involve
$y_{0:T}$, it passes through the conditional expectation unchanged and
\[
  \ell_\pi(\psi) = \bigl\{Q(\psi\mid\psi^{(i)}) - \pi(\beta)\bigr\}
                   - H(\psi\mid\psi^{(i)}).
\]
The first brace is maximized at $\psi^{(i+1)}$ by construction and the second
term is minimized at $\psi^{(i)}$, so the difference cannot decrease.
\end{proof}

The proposition is the reason the whole construction is cheap: the Kalman
filter and smoother, which are the expensive part of the algorithm, are not
touched at all. Only the point returned by the M-step changes.

\begin{remark}
Proposition~\ref{prop:monotone} concerns the pooled model. In the clustered
model the objective also carries the partition, which is updated by iterated
conditional modes, and the procedure is not globally monotone for reasons
unrelated to the penalty.
\end{remark}

\section{The M-step for the coefficients}\label{sec:mstep}

Conditionally on the other blocks, the part of $Q$ that involves $\beta$ is
\begin{equation}
  Q_\beta(\beta) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\beta' M \beta + \beta' v + \mathrm{const},
  \qquad
  M = \sum_{t=1}^{T} X_t'\Se^{-1}X_t,
  \quad
  v = \sum_{t=1}^{T} X_t'\Se^{-1}\hat v_t,
  \label{eq:Mv}
\end{equation}
where $\hat v_t = \hat z_t - K\hat y_t$ is the completed observation of period
$t$ net of the smoothed latent contribution --- ``completed'' because under
missing data $\hat z_t$ carries the conditional expectation of what was not
observed, and the conditional variance is added back into the second moments
elsewhere. The M-step therefore maximizes
\begin{equation}
  -\tfrac{1}{2}\beta'M\beta + \beta'v
  - \lambda\alpha\lVert D\beta\rVert_1
  - \tfrac{\lambda(1-\alpha)}{2}\beta'D\beta.
  \label{eq:mstepbeta}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Ridge: a closed form}

At $\alpha = 0$ the objective is a smooth concave quadratic and
\begin{equation}
  \hat\beta = \bigl( M + \lambda D \bigr)^{-1} v.
  \label{eq:ridge}
\end{equation}
$M$ and $v$ are already assembled by the M-step, and $r$ is small, so the cost
of \eqref{eq:ridge} over the unpenalized $M^{-1}v$ is one addition on a
diagonal.

\subsection{Lasso and elastic net: coordinate descent}

For $\alpha > 0$ the objective \eqref{eq:mstepbeta} is concave and its
non-smooth part is separable across coordinates, which is the setting in which
cyclic coordinate descent converges to a global maximizer
\citep{Tseng2001,Friedman2010}. Fixing all coordinates but the $j$th, the
first-order condition is
\[
  v_j - \sum_{l \ne j} M_{jl}\beta_l - M_{jj}\beta_j
  - \lambda(1-\alpha)w_j\beta_j
  - \lambda\alpha w_j\,\partial|\beta_j| \ni 0,
\]
whose solution is the soft-thresholding
\begin{equation}
  \beta_j \;\leftarrow\;
  \frac{\mathcal{S}\!\left(v_j - \sum_{l\ne j}M_{jl}\beta_l,\;
        \lambda\alpha w_j\right)}
       {M_{jj} + \lambda(1-\alpha)w_j},
  \qquad
  \mathcal{S}(x,\tau) = \sgn(x)\,(|x|-\tau)_+ .
  \label{eq:cd}
\end{equation}
An unpenalized coordinate has $w_j = 0$ and \eqref{eq:cd} reduces to its
ordinary conditional least squares update. Because the maximizer of the M-step
is attained exactly, \eqref{eq:mstep} is a genuine EM step and not merely a
generalized one; the cost is $O(r^2)$ per sweep on a matrix that is already
formed.

\subsection{The metric, and why the usual standardization is wrong here}
\label{sec:metric}

$M$ in \eqref{eq:Mv} is a \emph{generalized} least squares cross-product: it
carries $\Se^{-1}$, an estimated spatial covariance that changes from one EM
iteration to the next. The effective design is therefore
$\Se^{-1/2}X_t$ and not $X_t$, and standardizing the columns of $X$ to unit
Euclidean norm --- the default of every lasso implementation --- does not make
$\lambda$ comparable across covariates.

The scaling that does is the one that puts the diagonal of $M$ at one. Writing
$s_j = M_{jj}^{1/2}$, $S = \mathrm{diag}(s)$, $\tilde M = S^{-1}MS^{-1}$ and
$\tilde v = S^{-1}v$, the penalty is applied to $\tilde\beta = S\beta$ and the
solution returned on the original scale. Under this convention $\lambda$ is
measured in units of the GLS information each covariate carries, which is what
makes a single $\lambda$ meaningful across covariates and, in the clustered
model, across regimes of different sizes and different error covariances.

\begin{remark}
$s_j$ depends on $\Se$ and therefore changes between EM iterations. The scaling
is recomputed at each M-step. Since it is undone before the coefficients are
returned, the sequence of iterates is well defined; what is not constant across
iterations is the geometry in which a fixed $\lambda$ acts, and this is the
price of applying a penalty inside an algorithm that also estimates the metric.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The second scale, which applies to one half of the penalty}
\label{sec:scale2}

Removing the units of the covariates is not enough, and it is instructive that
the two halves of the elastic net need different treatment.

After the scaling of Section~\ref{sec:metric} the diagonal of $\tilde M$ is one,
so in the orthogonal case $\tilde M = I$ the ridge solution is
$\tilde\beta_j = \tilde v_j / (1+\lambda)$: the coefficient is multiplied by
$1/(1+\lambda)$ whatever the units of the response. Formally, replacing $z$ by
$cz$ sends $v \mapsto cv$ with $M$ and hence $S$ unchanged, so
$\tilde\beta \mapsto c\tilde\beta$ and $\beta \mapsto c\beta$, as it must;
replacing $\Se$ by $\sigma^2\Se$ sends $M \mapsto M/\sigma^2$,
$v \mapsto v/\sigma^2$, $s \mapsto s/\sigma$, hence $\tilde M$ unchanged,
$\tilde v \mapsto \tilde v/\sigma$, $\tilde\beta \mapsto \tilde\beta/\sigma$
and $\beta = S^{-1}\tilde\beta$ unchanged. \textbf{The $L_2$ part is already
free of the scale of the response.}

The $L_1$ part is not. Its parameter is a \emph{threshold} compared with the
gradient $\tilde v$, which carries those units: under
$\Se \mapsto \sigma^2\Se$ the soft-thresholding
$\mathcal{S}(\tilde v_j, \lambda\alpha)$ becomes
$\mathcal{S}(\tilde v_j/\sigma, \lambda\alpha)$, which is not
$\mathcal{S}(\tilde v_j, \lambda\alpha)/\sigma$. The same $\lambda$ therefore
means different things at different error variances and, in the clustered
model, in different regimes --- precisely what one does not want from a
hyperparameter that is to be shared.

The fix is to measure the $L_1$ part, and only it, against
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_{\mathrm{ref}} =
  \max_{j:\,w_j=1}\Bigl| \tilde v_j
    - \sum_{l:\,w_l=0} \tilde M_{jl}\,\tilde\beta_l^{\,0} \Bigr| ,
  \label{eq:lref}
\end{equation}
the largest partial gradient once the unpenalized coordinates have been
profiled out, $\tilde\beta^{\,0}$ being their conditional least squares
solution at $\tilde\beta_{\mathrm{pen}} = 0$. The M-step then applies
\begin{equation}
  \tau_j = \lambda\,\alpha\,\lambda_{\mathrm{ref}}\, w_j
  \quad\text{(threshold)}, \qquad
  \kappa_j = \lambda\,(1-\alpha)\, w_j
  \quad\text{(curvature)} .
  \label{eq:twoscales}
\end{equation}
Under \eqref{eq:twoscales} the estimator is invariant to the units of the
response for every $\alpha$, every penalized coefficient is exactly zero as
soon as $\lambda\alpha \ge 1$, and $\lambda \in (0,1]$ traverses the whole path
at $\alpha = 1$ --- the convention of \citet{Friedman2010}.

\begin{remark}
Scaling \emph{both} halves by $\lambda_{\mathrm{ref}}$, which is the obvious
thing to do and the first thing we did, breaks the ridge to fix the lasso: it
makes $\hat\beta(\lambda)$ depend on the error variance in a case where it need
not. On a design with a collinear pair the damage is of the order of
$10^{-1}$ in the coefficients, which is not small.
\end{remark}

\section{Effective degrees of freedom and the information criteria}

The information criteria used to select $k$ and the Potts penalty count free
parameters. With a penalty in force the nominal count $r$ overstates the
flexibility of the fit, and the effective number must replace it.

For the ridge, the fitted values are linear in the response with hat matrix
$H_\lambda = X(M+\lambda D)^{-1}X'\Se^{-1}$ in the GLS metric, so
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{df}(\lambda) = \tr\!\left\{ M\,(M+\lambda D)^{-1} \right\},
  \label{eq:dfridge}
\end{equation}
which decreases from $r$ at $\lambda = 0$ to the number of unpenalized
coordinates as $\lambda\to\infty$.

For the lasso, the number of non-zero coefficients is unbiased for the degrees
of freedom \citep{ZouHastieTibshirani2007}; for the elastic net the
corresponding quantity is \eqref{eq:dfridge} restricted to the active set
$\mathcal{A} = \{j : \hat\beta_j \ne 0\}$, with the ridge part of the penalty
only:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{df}(\lambda,\alpha) =
  \tr\!\left\{ M_{\mathcal{A}}\,
    \bigl(M_{\mathcal{A}} + \lambda(1-\alpha)D_{\mathcal{A}}\bigr)^{-1}\right\}.
  \label{eq:dfen}
\end{equation}
The criteria then read, for a fit with $k$ regimes,
\[
  \mathrm{BIC} = -2\hat\ell + \log(N)\Bigl\{
    \textstyle\sum_{g=1}^{k}\mathrm{df}_g(\lambda,\alpha) + k\,q \Bigr\},
\]
with $q$ the number of parameters of a regime other than the coefficients and
$N$ the number of observations, and analogously for AIC and KIC.

\begin{remark}
$\hat\ell$ is the \emph{unpenalized} log-likelihood evaluated at the penalized
estimate. Using the penalized objective in its place would double-count the
penalty, once through the objective and once through the degrees of freedom.
\end{remark}

\section{Which step comes first: the four hyperparameters}
\label{sec:order}

The procedure carries four quantities that are not estimated but chosen: the
number of regimes $k$, the strength $\varphi$ of the spatial penalty on the
partition, and the two of the elastic net. A natural question, and the one
practitioners ask first, is whether they can be selected in sequence --- fix
$(\alpha,\lambda)$ on the pooled fit, then look for $(k,\varphi)$, or the
reverse. They cannot, and this section says exactly why.

\subsection{The objective, and where each hyperparameter enters}

Write $g = (g_1,\dots,g_d)$ for the labels, $\mathcal{E}$ for the edge set of
the neighbourhood graph and $\ell_i(h;\Psi_h)$ for the contribution of location
$i$ under the parameters of regime $h$. The objective is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{Q}(g,\Psi;\, k,\varphi,\alpha,\lambda)
  = \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\ell_i(g_i;\Psi_{g_i})}_{\text{fit}}
  + \underbrace{\varphi\,c\!\!\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}}\!\!
      \mathbb{1}\{g_i=g_j\}}_{\text{Potts}}
  - \underbrace{\sum_{h=1}^{k}\pi_{\alpha,\lambda}(\beta_h)}_{\text{elastic net}} .
  \label{eq:objall}
\end{equation}
Three of the four are penalty weights inside \eqref{eq:objall} and change the
estimate; $k$ is the dimension of the model. The two penalties act on different
arguments, and that asymmetry is the whole content of the answer.

\begin{proposition}[The elastic net leaves the label step formally unchanged]
\label{prop:labels}
At fixed $\Psi$, the map $g \mapsto \mathcal{Q}(g,\Psi)$ differs from the
unpenalized one by the constant $\sum_h \pi_{\alpha,\lambda}(\beta_h)$. The
maximizer over $g$, and therefore the ICM update
\[
  g_i \;\leftarrow\;
  \argmax_{h} \Bigl\{ \ell_i(h;\Psi_h)
    + \varphi\,c \!\!\sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)}\!\! \mathbb{1}\{h = g_j\} \Bigr\},
\]
is unaffected.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Moving location $i$ from regime $h$ to regime $h'$ changes neither $\beta_h$
nor $\beta_{h'}$, which are held fixed in the label step, so the third term of
\eqref{eq:objall} is invariant and drops out of the arg-max.
\end{proof}

The penalty therefore reaches the partition only \emph{through} $\Psi$: the
labels are scored against shrunken coefficients. That is not a small channel.
Shrinkage moves every $\beta_h$ towards the same point, so the spread of
$\ell_i(\cdot)$ across regimes narrows, and with it the evidence any single
location carries about its own label.

\subsection{Three couplings, in the direction they act}

\paragraph{$\lambda$ depends on $k$.} At the optimum of predictive risk the
ridge parameter balances squared bias against variance. For a regime of $n_h$
locations the relevant information is $M_h = O(n_h T)$, so with $k$ balanced
regimes $M_h = O(dT/k)$ and, in the classical fixed-design expansion, the
risk-minimizing $\lambda$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \lambda^\star \;\asymp\;
  \frac{r\,\sigma^2}{\|\beta\|^2}\; \asymp\; \frac{1}{n_h}
  \;\asymp\; \frac{k}{d} .
  \label{eq:lamk}
\end{equation}
The optimal shrinkage \emph{grows} with $k$, and it does so at rate $k$. A
$\lambda$ calibrated on the pooled network is therefore too small by a factor
of about $k$ for the regimes it will be used in: it under-shrinks precisely
where shrinkage is needed.

\paragraph{$k$ depends on $\lambda$.} The criterion that selects $k$ charges
$\mathrm{df}(\lambda)$ per regime rather than $r$. From
\eqref{eq:dfridge}, $\mathrm{df}$ is decreasing in $\lambda$, so the marginal
cost of an extra regime,
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_k \;=\; \log(N)\bigl\{ \mathrm{df}(\lambda) + q \bigr\},
  \label{eq:margk}
\end{equation}
with $q$ the number of parameters of a regime other than the coefficients, is
\emph{decreasing} in $\lambda$ as well. Shrinkage makes an additional regime
cheaper and pushes the selected $k$ up. The two dependencies therefore run in
opposite directions and neither can be resolved without the other.

\paragraph{$\varphi$ depends on $\lambda$, through the calibration of its
scale.} The multiplier $c$ in \eqref{eq:objall} exists because
$\sum_i \ell_i$ and the count of concordant pairs are not on the same scale.
Calibrating it from the data, as the implementation does by default, sets
\begin{equation}
  \hat c \;=\;
  \frac{\mathrm{med}_i\bigl\{ \max_h \ell_i(h) - \min_h \ell_i(h) \bigr\}}
       {\overline{|\mathcal{N}|}} ,
  \label{eq:cscale}
\end{equation}
the typical gain from picking the best-fitting regime, per neighbour. By the
remark above the numerator is decreasing in $\lambda$, so $\hat c$ is too: a
\emph{fixed} $\varphi$ buys less spatial smoothing at a larger $\lambda$. Two
points of a grid indexed by $(\varphi,\lambda)$ are therefore not comparable in
$\varphi$ unless $c$ is held at a constant, which is what the \texttt{raw}
scaling of the implementation does.

\subsection{What follows for the search}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=3pt,topsep=3pt]
  \item[\textbf{Joint.}] Traverse the full grid
    $(k,\varphi,\alpha,\lambda)$ and select on it. Correct, and the cost is the
    product of the four grid sizes.
  \item[\textbf{Profiled, and what we recommend.}] For each $(k,\varphi)$,
    select $(\alpha,\lambda)$; then compare the $(k,\varphi)$ configurations
    \emph{each at its own} $(\hat\alpha,\hat\lambda)$. The cost is the same as
    the joint search, but the \emph{rule} is applied hierarchically, which
    matters because the rule for $\varphi$ is not a criterion at all: the Potts
    partition function $Z(\varphi,k)$ is intractable, and omitting it inverts
    the sign of the comparison, so $\varphi$ is settled by a stability argument
    on the partition rather than by an information criterion. The
    $(\alpha,\lambda)$ surface is flat relative to the $k$ surface, so a coarse
    inner grid --- $\alpha \in \{0,\tfrac12,1\}$ and $\lambda$ on a logarithmic
    scale --- suffices.
  \item[\textbf{Sequential, and why not.}] Fixing $(\alpha,\lambda)$ on the
    pooled fit and only then searching $(k,\varphi)$ is cheap and wrong in the
    specific direction of \eqref{eq:lamk}: it applies the shrinkage appropriate
    to $d$ locations inside regimes of $d/k$.
\end{description}

\subsection{Which likelihood the criterion sees}

One point deserves stating on its own, because the natural guess is the wrong
one. It is tempting to select $(k,\varphi)$ on the objective
\eqref{eq:objall} \emph{including} the elastic-net term --- the penalized
likelihood, augmented by the regularization. That double-counts. The effect of
the penalty on the flexibility of the fit is already carried by
$\mathrm{df}(\lambda,\alpha)$ in
\eqref{eq:dfridge}--\eqref{eq:dfen}; subtracting $\pi_{\alpha,\lambda}$ from
the log-likelihood as well charges for it twice, once through the objective and
once through the dimension. The construction that is consistent with
\citet{ZouHastieTibshirani2007} is
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{IC} = -2\,\hat\ell\bigl(\hat\Psi(\lambda,\alpha)\bigr)
    + \mathrm{pen}(N)\Bigl\{ \textstyle\sum_h \mathrm{df}_h(\lambda,\alpha) + kq \Bigr\},
  \label{eq:icfinal}
\end{equation}
with $\hat\ell$ the \emph{unpenalized} log-likelihood evaluated at the
\emph{penalized} estimate.

The coherent alternative is Bayesian: read $\pi_{\alpha,\lambda}$ as a prior ---
Gaussian for the ridge, Laplace for the lasso --- and select on the marginal
likelihood. That requires the normalizing constant of the prior, which for
those two is available in closed form. It is not available for the Potts term,
whose constant $Z(\varphi,k)$ is the intractable one, so a procedure that
marginalized over $\beta$ while conditioning on $\varphi$ would be applying two
different logics to two penalties of the same objective. We do not pursue it.

\section{Choosing $\lambda$ and $\alpha$ in practice}

Two routes are available and they do not agree in general.

\paragraph{Information criteria.} Cheap, since the grid is already being
traversed for $(k,\varphi)$, and consistent with the rest of the selection
apparatus once \eqref{eq:dfridge}--\eqref{eq:dfen} are used. The known
difficulty is that in-sample criteria on a model whose partition is itself
estimated are optimistic, and the optimism grows with $k$.

\paragraph{Spatio-temporal cross-validation.} The honest route, and the one we
recommend when the computational budget allows it. The blocking scheme matters:
random $K$-fold over the $(t,i)$ cells is anti-conservative here because the
latent process and the spatial field make neighbouring cells nearly duplicates.
Leave-$L$-locations-out, leave-$H$-times-out and the combined
leave-$L$-locations-and-$H$-times-out schemes address the two dependencies
separately and jointly; see \citet{OttoFassoMaranzano2024} for the review and
\citet{Meyer2018} for the implementation these follow. The predictive quantity
to score is the fitted value at held-out cells, which the package computes
through the conditional expectation given the observed sub-vector.

\section{Inference}

The parametric bootstrap already available for the model generates a replicate
from the fitted parameters and re-estimates. With a penalty two changes are
required.

First, if $(\lambda,\alpha)$ were selected from the data, the selection must be
repeated inside every bootstrap draw. Otherwise the intervals condition on a
tuning parameter that is itself an estimate, in exactly the way that intervals
conditioning on an estimated partition understate the uncertainty of the
clustered model.

Second, when $\alpha > 0$ the estimator is not smooth and the sampling
distribution of a coefficient at the boundary is not asymptotically normal; the
percentile and bias-corrected intervals remain usable as descriptions of the
bootstrap distribution but do not have their usual coverage interpretation.
Post-selection inference for the active set requires the conditional machinery
of \citet{LeeSunSunTaylor2016}, which we do not develop here. For a ridge,
$\alpha = 0$, the estimator is smooth and this difficulty does not arise,
though the usual bias remains.

\section{The clustered model}
\label{sec:scstem}

Within a regime, everything above applies verbatim with $M_g$, $v_g$ and
$\Se^{(g)}$ in place of their pooled counterparts. Three points are specific to
the clustered case.

\paragraph{A common $\lambda$ across regimes is meaningful only after scaling.}
Regimes differ in size and in error covariance, so $M_g$ differs in magnitude
between them. The scalings of Sections~\ref{sec:metric} and \ref{sec:scale2}
are what make a single $(\lambda,\alpha)$ interpretable across the partition;
without them the penalty would bind hardest on the smallest regime for a reason
that has nothing to do with its collinearity.

\subsection{Pooled, size-scaled, or genuinely cluster-specific}
\label{sec:pooled}

Three regimes of increasing flexibility have to be distinguished, and the
companion paper and this one sit at opposite ends of the range.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=3pt,topsep=3pt]
  \item[\textbf{Pooled.}] One $(\alpha,\lambda)$ for the whole partition. Under
    the scalings above this already means the same \emph{proportional}
    shrinkage in every regime, because $\lambda_{\mathrm{ref}}$ in
    \eqref{eq:lref} is computed inside each regime and therefore carries its own
    information: a regime with half the locations has a proportionally smaller
    reference, and the same $\lambda$ buys the same fraction of the path. This
    is the configuration of the companion paper, and it has the decisive
    practical advantage of adding \emph{one} number to a selection problem that
    already has three.
  \item[\textbf{Size-scaled.}] $\lambda_g = \lambda\,\bar n / n_g$ with
    $\bar n = d/k$. Still one hyperparameter, but the shrinkage is deliberately
    made stronger in the small regimes, on the argument that their coefficients
    are noisier than proportionality alone accounts for --- which is what
    \eqref{eq:lamk} says, with $n_h$ in place of $d/k$. This is the natural
    middle term and is what we would try first.
  \item[\textbf{Cluster-specific.}] $(\alpha_g,\lambda_g)$ free in every regime.
    This is the object of the present paper, and it is not a small step: the
    grid grows as $|\Lambda|^k$, the per-regime selection is unstable when
    $n_g$ is small, and comparability breaks, since a regime can improve its
    own fit by choosing less shrinkage. What makes it worth the trouble is that
    the amount of regularization a regime needs is a feature of that regime ---
    a dense urban sub-network and a sparse rural one do not have the same
    collinearity --- so forcing one $\lambda$ on both is a restriction, and one
    that the data can speak about.
\end{description}

The open questions are which of the three the selection can actually support at
realistic $(d, T, k)$, and whether a hierarchical formulation --- $\lambda_g$
drawn around a common $\lambda$, with one variance to select rather than $k$
values --- recovers most of the flexibility at the cost of the pooled version.

\paragraph{The penalty interacts with the minimum regime size.} The lower bound
on $n_g$ exists because a regime must support its own parameters. A penalty
reduces the effective number of coefficients, and with it the bound. This is the
most substantive consequence for practice: it is what would allow finer
partitions to be entertained on a network of a given size.

\paragraph{A fused alternative.} The penalty considered here shrinks each
$\beta_g$ towards zero independently. An alternative, arguably better matched to
the clustered model, shrinks them towards each other,
\[
  \pi_{\mathrm{fused}}(\beta_1,\dots,\beta_k)
  = \lambda \sum_{g < g'} \nu_{gg'} \lVert D(\beta_g - \beta_{g'})\rVert,
\]
with $\nu_{gg'}$ a weight, possibly spatial. That penalty addresses the
smallness of the regimes rather than the collinearity of the covariates, it
couples the M-steps of different regimes, and it makes the number of distinct
coefficient vectors an outcome of the fit rather than a choice. It is a
different paper.

\section{Penalized linear regression as a special case}
\label{sec:lm}

The construction contains the ordinary elastic net, and it is worth saying
exactly how, both because it fixes the interpretation of $\lambda$ and because
it is the check the implementation can be held to.

Two components have to be switched off. Setting $K = 0$ in \eqref{eq:meas}
removes the latent process: the filter gain is zero, the smoothed states are
the prior propagated forward, and $G$, $\Sigma_\eta$ and $m_0$ are
unidentified, so they are held fixed rather than updated. Replacing
$C(h;\theta)$ by the identity in \eqref{eq:cov} removes the spatial
correlation, leaving $\Se = \sigma^2 I_d$ with $\sigma^2 = \sigma^2_\varepsilon
+ \sigma^2_\omega$ the only identified combination, and the Newton--Raphson
step, which would be estimating a range that has nothing to estimate, is
skipped. What remains is
\begin{equation}
  z_{ti} = x_{ti}'\beta + e_{ti},
  \qquad e \sim N(0, \sigma^2 I),
  \label{eq:lm}
\end{equation}
and the M-step \eqref{eq:Mv} becomes $M = X'X/\sigma^2$, $v = X'z/\sigma^2$.

The factor $\sigma^{-2}$ cancels: it divides $M$ and $v$ alike, and both
scalings of Sections~\ref{sec:metric} and \ref{sec:scale2} are invariant to it.
The estimator is therefore \emph{exactly} the elastic net on $X$ and $z$, and
the EM converges in one M-step for $\beta$ given $\sigma^2$.

\begin{remark}
One detail decides whether the agreement is exact or merely close. The
implementation adds a small ridge to the diagonal of every matrix it inverts,
for conditioning; on a design with a collinear pair that ridge is comparable
with the smallest eigenvalue of $X'X$ in the offending direction, and it moves
the coefficients by an amount of order $10^{-2}$. Setting it to zero brings the
agreement with a direct elastic net to between $10^{-14}$ and $10^{-11}$ for
the ridge, the lasso, the elastic net and the unpenalized case alike. A
tolerance that is invisible in a well-conditioned problem is not invisible in
the problem the penalty exists for.
\end{remark}

With $k > 1$ and the same two switches the construction gives \emph{clusterwise}
penalized regression, the partition still estimated from the data by the Potts
term --- which is the cross-sectional model of the spatially-clustered
regression literature, recovered here as the degenerate case of a
spatio-temporal one.

\section{Algorithm}

\begin{quote}\small
\textbf{input} data $z$, design $X$, coordinates, starting $\psi^{(0)}$,
$(\alpha,\lambda)$, weights $w$\\[2pt]
\textbf{repeat} for $i = 0,1,2,\dots$\\
\hspace*{1em} 1. E-step: Kalman filter and smoother at $\psi^{(i)}$; form
$\hat y_t$, $C^s_t$, $\hat z_t$\\
\hspace*{1em} 2. M-step, other blocks: closed forms for $\sigma^2_\omega$,
$G$, $\Sig_\eta$, $m_0$; Newton--Raphson for $(\theta,\sigma^2_\varepsilon)$\\
\hspace*{1em} 3. M-step, coefficients:\\
\hspace*{2em} (a) assemble $M$, $v$ as in \eqref{eq:Mv}\\
\hspace*{2em} (b) $s_j \leftarrow M_{jj}^{1/2}$;
$\tilde M \leftarrow S^{-1}MS^{-1}$, $\tilde v \leftarrow S^{-1}v$\\
\hspace*{2em} (c) if $\alpha = 0$: $\tilde\beta \leftarrow
(\tilde M + \lambda D)^{-1}\tilde v$\\
\hspace*{2em} \phantom{(c)} else: cycle \eqref{eq:cd} over $j$ until
convergence\\
\hspace*{2em} (d) $\beta^{(i+1)} \leftarrow S^{-1}\tilde\beta$;
record $\mathrm{df}$ from \eqref{eq:dfridge} or \eqref{eq:dfen}\\
\textbf{until} the relative changes in $\psi$ and in $\ell_\pi$ are both below
the tolerance
\end{quote}

The stopping rule needs one adjustment when $\alpha > 0$: the relative criterion
on the parameter vector is undefined at a coordinate that has just become
exactly zero, and the active set may change from one iteration to the next
without the objective moving appreciably. The implementation floors the
denominator and tests the objective as well as the parameters.

\section{What remains to be done}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Simulation evidence.} The design of the companion software paper
provides the machinery: what is missing is a factor for the collinearity of the
covariates, which is the quantity the penalty is supposed to address and which
that design holds fixed at a single covariate.
\item \textbf{Consistency and rates.} Under what conditions on $T$, $d$, and the
decay of the spatial correlation is $\hat\beta(\lambda)$ consistent, and at what
rate? The effective sample size is not $Td$: the spatial field and the latent
process both reduce it, and the reduction depends on $\theta$ and $G$.
\item \textbf{The degrees of freedom of \eqref{eq:dfen}} are the fixed-design
ones. Here the metric is estimated, and whether the correction matters at
realistic $T$ is an open question we have not examined.
\item \textbf{Selection consistency} of the lasso part in the presence of an
estimated spatial covariance, and whether the irrepresentable condition can be
stated in the GLS metric.
\item \textbf{The fused penalty} of Section~\ref{sec:scstem}, which is a
separate development and probably the more interesting one.
\end{enumerate}
